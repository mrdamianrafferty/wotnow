# Anti-Smugness Check

**Read this when:** reviewing any draft before publication. Smugness is the dominant failure mode of UK gardening writing. The fix is usually to remove the line rather than to soften it.

## The five-question check

Before publishing any prose, read it back and ask:

1. **Does it talk down to the reader?** (lecturing tone, "of course", "as any gardener knows", "needless to say")
2. **Does it gatekeep?** (class-marker language, "the proper gardener does…", reference to estates / dedicated growers as a standard)
3. **Does it show off?** (performative Latin in opening paragraphs, name-dropping famous gardens for borrowed authority)
4. **Does it hedge to preen?** ("Though I prefer to do it differently myself…")
5. **Does it use words the reader won't use?** (corporate "transform", "leverage", "harness", "unlock", "elevate")

Any "yes" means rewrite. The fix is usually to remove the line rather than to soften it.

## Words and phrases that signal smugness

Strike these on sight unless the surrounding text makes them load-bearing (rare):

- "Of course"
- "Naturally"
- "Needless to say"
- "Obviously"
- "As any gardener knows"
- "The dedicated grower"
- "The serious gardener"
- "A proper kitchen garden"
- "Simply" (when used to mean "all you have to do")
- "Just" (used the same way)
- "Effortlessly"
- "Truly" / "really" as intensifiers

## Worked rewrites

**Smug:**
> The dedicated grower will, of course, want to bring their rhubarb crowns into production as early as possible. Forcing — a technique practised for generations on the great Yorkshire estates — produces those tender pink stalks that are the mark of a proper kitchen garden.

**Direct rewrite:**
> Force rhubarb for an earlier, sweeter crop. Cover one crown in late January with a tall pot or bucket, blocking the light. The stems push up pale pink and tender in four to six weeks. The crown needs a year off afterwards.

**Smug:**
> Naturally, one would simply leave the side-shoots intact on bush varieties — only the dedicated cordon grower needs to pinch out.

**Direct rewrite:**
> Bush tomatoes don't need side-shoots removed. Cordon varieties do — pinch them out weekly from late June.

**Smug:**
> Of course, the truly committed gardener will compost their own kitchen waste rather than rely on shop-bought soil improvers.

**Direct rewrite:**
> Home compost is cheaper than bagged soil improver and arguably better — but bagged compost works fine if you don't have the space for a heap.

## Latin name smugness

Latin in opening paragraphs is the most common form of showing off. The pattern: a perfectly clear common name is supplied with its binomial in the first sentence, as a quiet flex. Strike it.

**Smug:**
> Rhubarb (*Rheum × hybridum*), a perennial vegetable of the family Polygonaceae, is among the most reliable crops for the British kitchen garden…

**Direct rewrite:**
> Rhubarb is among the most reliable crops you can grow. The crowns last twenty years if you look after them…

(The Latin name belongs in the Quick Facts card or H1 element, not the opening sentence of a body paragraph.)

## When borrowed authority is fine

Citing **specific evidence** is not smug. "A 2019 RHS trial found…", "the Soil Association notes…", "Charles Dowding records harvests of…" — these are sources, and naming them is honest.

Citing **estates and famous gardens** for borrowed authority ("a technique practised on the great Yorkshire estates", "the lawns at Sissinghurst") is smug because the authority is about prestige, not evidence.

## When humility tips into preening

Watch for the move that goes: assert a position, then humble-brag about being unusual for holding it.

**Preening:**
> I prefer to leave my beds undug, though I'm aware this puts me at odds with most allotment-holders…

**Direct:**
> No-dig works well on most soils and saves a lot of time.

## The cumulative check

Voice consistency compounds. A single smug sentence on a single page is recoverable; the same one across a thousand plant pages becomes the brand. Worth being strict.
