# No-dig and Organic Principles

**Read this when:** writing about no-dig method, organic principles, mulching, composting, green manures, crop rotation, or any soil-management technique. Also when writing about Dowding specifically.

## The Charles Dowding no-dig method

The Charles Dowding method (no-dig, compost-mulched beds) genuinely produces good yields with less weeding and less effort. Key points if writing about it:

- Compost goes on top, not dug in. 2–3 cm per year on established beds; 10–15 cm to start a new bed over grass.
- Weeds are suppressed by mulch, not pulled. Perennial weed roots (bindweed, couch, ground elder) need cardboard underneath for the first year.
- Soil structure is preserved by not turning it — root channels, fungal networks, worm tunnels all stay intact.
- Watering is needed but the mulch retains moisture longer than bare soil.
- Pests still happen — slugs love mulch. No-dig isn't a pest-management system.

If quoting Dowding directly, reference his books or [charlesdowding.co.uk](https://charlesdowding.co.uk/). Don't put words in his mouth.

## Organic principles for Grow Daisy content

- Build soil fertility through compost, green manures, and minimal disturbance.
- Use cultural and biological controls before any chemical input. Encourage predators (ladybirds, hoverflies, lacewings, parasitic wasps, frogs, birds).
- Where a chemical is needed, prefer those certified for organic use (ferric phosphate, copper, sulphur, soft soap, *Bacillus thuringiensis*).
- Crop rotation breaks pest and disease cycles. The standard 3- or 4-year rotation groups crops by family: legumes / brassicas / roots / others.

## When organic isn't the assumed frame

Grow Daisy users include conventional gardeners. When advising:

- If the user asks for a chemical control, give it; flag whether it's approved for organic use.
- If the user asks for an organic approach, give it without lecturing.
- Don't moralise about pesticides. Describe what they do and let the user choose.

## Composting in brief

- A working compost heap needs roughly 50:50 green (nitrogen — grass clippings, vegetable peelings, manure) to brown (carbon — autumn leaves, cardboard, straw).
- Turn it or don't — turned heaps finish faster (3–6 months), unturned heaps take longer (12–18 months) but produce comparable compost.
- Leaf mould is a separate slow process — pile autumn leaves alone, 18–24 months.

## Green manures

Common UK choices:

- **Field bean (Vicia faba)** — autumn-sown, dug in or chopped down in spring. Adds nitrogen.
- **Phacelia** — summer or autumn, mineral conditioner.
- **Crimson clover (Trifolium incarnatum)** — summer or autumn, nitrogen fixer.
- **Rye (Secale cereale)** — autumn, strong root system to break compaction.
- **Mustard** — fast-growing, brassica family so do not use in brassica beds the following year.
