## Section 1: Plant Families and Rotation Logic

### Why Families Matter

Crop rotation works on the principle that **different plant families have different soil needs, pest preferences, and disease susceptibility**. By rotating families, you:
- Prevent soil nutrient depletion (heavy feeders → nitrogen-fixers → light feeders)
- Avoid pest/disease buildup in soil (soil-borne pathogens target specific families)
- Break pest life cycles (insects overwinter waiting for their host family)
- Build soil structure naturally through different root depths and organic matter

### The Major Plant Families (with Common & Botanical Names)

**Family 1: Brassicas (Cruciferae / Brassicaceae)**
- Common names: Cabbage family, Cole crops
- Includes: Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, brussels sprouts, radish, turnip, pak choi, mizuna, mustard
- Character: Heavy feeders (nitrogen), susceptible to clubroot (soil-borne fungus), cabbage white butterfly, flea beetles
- Soil depth: Shallow-medium (root depth 30-45cm)
- Key trait: Require well-draining soil to prevent clubroot; benefit from lime

**Family 2: Solanaceae (Nightshade family)**
- Common names: Nightshades, fruiting crops
- Includes: Tomato, potato, pepper, eggplant, chilli
- Character: Heavy feeders, susceptible to early/late blight, verticillium wilt (soil-borne), whitefly
- Soil depth: Medium-deep (root depth 45-60cm for tomatoes, 30cm for potatoes)
- Key trait: Avoid soil-borne fungal diseases; never plant potato then tomato in same bed
- **Climate note:** Susceptible to blight in wet Atlantic conditions

**Family 3: Alliums (Alliaceae)**
- Common names: Onion family
- Includes: Onion, garlic, leek, shallot, chives, spring onion
- Character: Light-moderate feeders, allelopathic (suppress other plants), pest-resistant, store long-term
- Soil depth: Shallow-medium (root depth 25-40cm)
- Key trait: Often used as a transitional/cleaning crop; low disease pressure

**Family 4: Legumes (Fabaceae)**
- Common names: Pea and bean family
- Includes: Pea, broad bean, runner bean, French bean, chickpea, lentil
- Character: **Light feeders but nitrogen FIXERS** (add 50-100 kg N/ha to soil); also deep-rooting
- Soil depth: Medium-deep (root depth 40-90cm depending on type)
- Key trait: Break pest cycles naturally; precede heavy feeders; improve soil structure
- **Climate note:** Excellent for Atlantic conditions; double-cropping possible (spring peas + summer beans)

**Family 5: Cucurbits (Cucurbitaceae)**
- Common names: Melon/squash family, vine crops
- Includes: Courgette, cucumber, pumpkin, melon, squash, marrow
- Character: Heavy feeders, hungry for nitrogen and potassium, sprawling
- Soil depth: Deep (root depth 60-90cm)
- Key trait: Benefit from well-composted soil; susceptible to powdery mildew (humidity-loving in wet climates)
- **Climate note:** Short season in Atlantic regions; mulching critical

**Family 6: Chenopodiaceae (Amaranthaceae - formerly)**
- Common names: Beet family, goosefoot family
- Includes: Beet (beetroot), spinach, chard, quinoa, amaranth
- Character: Light feeders, tolerant, hardy, deep-rooting
- Soil depth: Deep (root depth 45-60cm)
- Key trait: Often used as "cleaning" or transitional crops; improve soil color/texture

**Family 7: Umbelliferae (Apiaceae)**
- Common names: Carrot family, parsnip family, herb family
- Includes: Carrot, parsnip, celery, fennel, coriander, parsley, dill, lovage
- Character: Light-medium feeders, deep-rooting (especially parsnip/salsify), long season
- Soil depth: Deep (root depth 45-90cm depending on type)
- Key trait: Improve soil structure; slow germination; few pests

**Family 8: Asteraceae (formerly Compositae)**
- Common names: Daisy/sunflower family
- Includes: Lettuce, endive, chicory, artichoke, sunflower
- Character: Light feeders, quick-growing, shallow-rooting
- Soil depth: Shallow (root depth 20-35cm)
- Key trait: Perfect for catch crops between main rotations; frequent harvesting

### Quick Reference: Family Groups for Simplified Rotation

**Option A: Simplified 4-Family System (recommended for most home gardeners)**
1. **Family A (Heavy Feeders):** Brassicas + Cucurbits (can be combined)
2. **Family B (Nitrogen Fixers):** Legumes
3. **Family C (Light Feeders):** Alliums + Umbelliferae + Chenopodiaceae (roots & storage crops)
4. **Family D (Soil Builders):** Asteraceae + leafy greens (composted back into beds)

**Option B: Traditional 3-Family System (stricter)**
1. **Brassicas** (cabbage, broccoli, kale, radish)
2. **Solanaceae + Cucurbits** (tomato, potato, pepper, courgette, squash) - combined as "hungry crops"
3. **Legumes + Umbelliferae + Chenopodiaceae** (peas, beans, carrots, beets, spinach)

## Section 2: Rotation Rules and Logic

### The Core "Never Follow" Rules

These are biological/chemical rules that matter significantly:

1. **Never plant Solanaceae after Solanaceae**
   - Why: Soil-borne pathogens (early blight, late blight, verticillium wilt, Phytophthora) persist 3-5 years
   - This means: No tomato → tomato, potato → tomato, pepper → potato
   - Exception: Different species in different years IF you've had no disease problems
   - Atlantic climate impact: Wet conditions make blight worse; 4-5 year gap preferred

2. **Never plant Brassica after Brassica (or skip 2 years)**
   - Why: Clubroot (Plasmodiophora brassicae) soil-borne fungus can persist 10-15 years once established
   - This means: No cabbage → cabbage, kale → broccoli, radish → cabbage
   - Severity: Clubroot is a persistent threat; once established, rotations must be STRICT
   - Prevention: Liming to pH 7.0+ reduces clubroot risk

3. **Never plant Alliums after Alliums**
   - Why: Pink root disease, white rot, onion white rot persist in soil
   - This means: No garlic → onion, leek → garlic (2-3 year minimum)
   - Note: Alliums are generally low-disease, but isolation helps

4. **Legumes before Heavy Feeders (not a hard rule, but optimal)**
   - Why: Legume nitrogen fixation directly benefits the next crop's uptake
   - Timing: Plant legumes, harvest in mid-summer, plant heavy feeders (brassicas) in late summer/fall for winter harvest
   - This means: Peas (spring/early summer) → nitrogen-heavy brassicas (autumn/winter crops)

5. **Avoid following Potato with Tomato or Pepper**
   - Why: Both are Solanaceae; potato leaves behind blight spores and verticillium wilt
   - Severity: High; this is the single most important "never" rule in home gardens
   - Wait: Minimum 4 years, preferably 5 in wet climates

### Why Legumes Precede Brassicas (The Nitrogen Story)

**The biological mechanism:**
1. Legume roots house Rhizobium bacteria in nodules; bacteria fix atmospheric N₂ into ammonia
2. When legumes are harvested, the nitrogen-rich foliage is removed BUT root nodules decompose in soil
3. This releases bioavailable nitrogen (NH₄⁺) that the next crop can immediately use
4. Brassicas are nitrogen-hungry (50-100 kg N/ha) and benefit directly

**Practical example in Atlantic Spain:**
- April-May: Plant peas (early spring variety) in Bed A
- July: Harvest peas, remove foliage, leave roots in soil
- August: Plant autumn brassicas (kale, cauliflower, winter cabbage) in Bed A
- October-February: Harvest brassicas; they benefited from legume nitrogen fixation

**Why this matters for the app:**
- Users should see a "high compatibility" suggestion when planning legume → brassica
- Calculate nitrogen benefit when suggesting follow-on crops

### "Friendly Follows" (Synergistic Combinations)

These combinations have biological or structural benefits:

1. **Heavy feeder → Light feeder → Nitrogen fixer**
   - Natural progression: Brassica (draws down soil N) → Carrot/onion (light feeder) → Legume (restores N)
   - Repeats every 3 years

2. **Deep roots → Shallow roots → Medium roots**
   - Breaks hardpan, improves structure, prevents compaction
   - Example: Parsnip (deep) → Lettuce (shallow) → Brassica (medium)

3. **Legumes + Asteraceae as soil builders**
   - Both can be composted back into the bed rather than harvested as much
   - Improves organic matter: Key for Atlantic clay soils

4. **Alliums as "cleaning crops"**
   - Alliums have allelopathic properties (suppress weeds/pests)
   - Often used between heavy rotations to reset soil ecology
   - Example: Heavy feeder → Allium (cleaning year) → Legume

## Section 3: Mediterranean/Atlantic Climate Considerations

### Why Atlantic Climate Changes Rotation Timing

**Atlantic/Oceanic Climate Profile (Asturias, UK, Brittany):**
- Mild winters (rarely below 0°C)
- High rainfall (800-1500mm/year)
- High humidity
- Short, cool summers (15-18°C average)
- Long growing season (October-April for winter crops)
- Blight-favorable conditions (wet, moderate temps)

### Key Climate Impacts on Rotation

#### 1. Extended Winter Growing Season
- **Opportunity:** Year-round rotation possible
- Typical pattern:
  - Spring (Feb-Apr): Legumes (peas, broad beans)
  - Summer (May-Aug): Cucurbits + Solanaceae (tomato, courgette)
  - Autumn (Sep-Oct): Plant brassicas for winter harvest
  - Winter (Nov-Feb): Harvest brassicas, fallow or plant garlic/onions
- **App implication:** Can track 4-5 distinct cropping periods per year, not just 1-2

#### 2. Blight Pressure is HIGH
- **Diseases:** Phytophthora infestans (late blight on tomato/potato), Phytophthora fragariae (red core in strawberry)
- **Conditions:** Cool nights + high humidity = perfect blight conditions
- **Rotation impact:**
  - Never follow potato with tomato/pepper (mandatory 5-year gap)
  - Solanaceae should be rotated at least 4 years minimum
  - Brassicas similarly susceptible to downy mildew in high humidity
- **User guidance:** Suggest copper fungicide or resistant varieties as companion advice

#### 3. Soil Type: Often Heavy Clay
- **Drainage issues:** Clayey soils common in Atlantic regions; waterlogging risk
- **Rotation benefit:** Legume deep roots and Umbelliferae root penetration help break clay
- **User guidance:**
  - Suggest legumes for clay-breaking effect
  - Compost heavy feeders (brassicas, cucurbits) back into clay to improve structure
  - Warn about clubroot risk in acid, wet clay (encourage liming)

#### 4. Nitrogen Mineralization is Slow
- **Reason:** Cool temps, wet soil = slow decomposition of organic matter
- **Implication:**
  - Nitrogen fixed by legumes is released slowly over winter (good; matches slow uptake)
  - Heavy feeders planted in autumn (brassicas) will see nitrogen benefit through winter
  - More legume nitrogen "sticks around" in Atlantic climate than in warmer regions

#### 5. Pest Pressure is Lower (but different)
- **Low:** Aphids, whitefly (too cold much of year)
- **High:** Clubroot, slugs, slugs, and more slugs (wet conditions)
- **Rotation implication:** Slug damage is universal; rotation doesn't prevent it, but legumes may encourage slug predators

### Recommended Rotation Pattern for Atlantic Climate

**Year 1 Pattern (4-bed rotation):**

| Bed | Spring (Feb-May) | Summer (Jun-Aug) | Autumn (Sep-Oct) | Winter (Nov-Feb) |
|-----|---|---|---|---|
| **Bed A** | Peas, broad beans | Fallow/cover crop | Kale, winter cabbage | Harvest/fallow |
| **Bed B** | Spinach, chard | Tomatoes, courgettes | Onions, garlic plant | Harvest/harvest |
| **Bed C** | Lettuce (catch crop) | Potatoes (skip if blight risk) | Carrots, beets | Harvest/fallow |
| **Bed D** | Onions planted | Leafy brassicas (summer cabbages) | Legume catch crop (clover) | Fallow/prepare |

**Year 2:** Rotate each bed clockwise (A→B→C→D→A)

**Key principle:** Legumes (Spring) → Heavy feeders (Autumn/Winter). This allows nitrogen from decomposing legume roots to support hungry brassicas over the long winter growing season.

## Section 4: Practical Simplification for Small Gardens

### The Minimum Viable Rotation (2-4 Beds)

Most home gardeners have 2-4 beds. A strict 3-year rotation is hard with only 2 beds (you'd need to fallow half your garden). Here's a practical minimum:

#### For 2-Bed Garden

**Simple 3-Year Rotation (rotate every 3 years):**

**Year 1:**
- Bed A: Heavy feeders (brassicas, cucurbits, tomatoes)
- Bed B: Legumes + light feeders (peas, beans, carrots, onions)

**Year 2:**
- Bed A: Legumes + light feeders
- Bed B: Heavy feeders

**Year 3:**
- Bed A: Heavy feeders
- Bed B: Legumes + light feeders

**Problem:** You're repeating the same family in same bed every 2 years (inadequate for some pests).

**Better 3-Year Rotation for 2 Beds:**

**Year 1:**
- Bed A: Spring legumes (peas) → Summer heavy feeders (tomato, courgette)
- Bed B: Brassicas (winter harvest) → Onions (spring plant)

**Year 2:**
- Bed A: Alliums (onions, garlic) → Winter brassicas
- Bed B: Spring legumes (peas, beans) → Summer tomatoes

**Year 3:**
- Back to Year 1 pattern

**Key advantage:** Each family gets 2 years minimum away from each bed. Not perfect, but adequate for most home gardeners.

---

#### For 3-Bed Garden (RECOMMENDED for home gardeners)

**Strict 3-Year Rotation:**

| Year | Bed A | Bed B | Bed C |
|-----|---|---|---|
| **1** | Brassicas | Legumes | Alliums + Umbelliferae |
| **2** | Alliums + Umbelliferae | Brassicas | Legumes |
| **3** | Legumes | Alliums + Umbelliferae | Brassicas |
| **4** | Back to Year 1 | | |

This ensures:
- Each family has 2 years away from each bed
- Legume nitrogen goes to hungry brassicas (Year 2)
- Simple to remember and track

**Seasonal variation within each bed (optional, advanced):**
- Summer crop + winter crop in same bed, different families
- Example Bed A Year 1: Summer brassicas (cabbage) + Winter onion plants
- But for app purposes, simplify: track by bed and year

---

#### For 4-Bed Garden (IDEAL)

**4-Year Rotation (very clean):**

| Year | Bed A | Bed B | Bed C | Bed D |
|-----|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Brassicas | Legumes | Umbelliferae | Alliums |
| **2** | Alliums | Brassicas | Legumes | Umbelliferae |
| **3** | Umbelliferae | Alliums | Brassicas | Legumes |
| **4** | Legumes | Umbelliferae | Alliums | Brassicas |
| **5** | Back to Year 1 | | | |

This ensures:
- Each family has 3 years away from each bed
- Long rotation breaks all soil-borne pests
- Legume→Brassica succession happens naturally

### The "Real Minimum" for 2 Beds with Mixed Crops

If users insist on not rotating:

**Acceptable compromise:** Plant different families within the same bed, tracking location.

Example Bed Layout (3m × 1m bed):
- Section 1 (0-1m): Tomato (Solanaceae) Year 1
- Section 2 (1-2m): Carrot (Umbelliferae) Year 1
- Section 3 (2-3m): Pea (Legume) Year 1

Year 2, rotate sections:
- Section 1: Carrot (away from tomato)
- Section 2: Pea (away from carrot)
- Section 3: Tomato (away from pea)

**App implication:** For very small beds or mixed planting, allow **sub-bed rotation tracking** (user divides bed into zones, rotates zones).

## Section 5: Mixed Beds and Non-Rotation Guidance

### Should Mixed Beds Exist?

**Horticultural answer:** Rotation is optimal, but mixed beds have value for:
1. Small gardens (spatial constraints)
2. Companion planting (three sisters: corn, beans, squash)
3. Continuous harvest (lettuce + roots)
4. Aesthetic/kitchen garden traditions

**Real benefits even without rotation:**
- Companion planting (borage + tomato, marigold + brassica)
- Trap crops (nasturtium attracts aphids away from brassicas)
- Pest suppression (mixed plantings reduce monoculture collapse risk)

### Guidance for Mixed Beds

**App approach:**
1. Allow mixed beds (don't force rotation)
2. **Track what was planted where** (for future rotation if user wants it)
3. Provide warnings for "risky" combinations
4. Educate gradually (show benefits of rotation, don't mandate)

**Specific warnings the app should show:**
- "Tomato was in this bed last year. Plant tomato again? (Blight risk if wet season)" → Warn, allow anyway
- "Garlic in this bed last year. Onions this year? (Share diseases)" → Suggest 1-year gap
- "Potato last year, tomato this year? Avoid this" → Strong warning

### Data Tracking for Non-Rotating Gardens

Even if users don't formally rotate, tracking history enables:
1. Pattern detection ("I always have blight in this bed")
2. Gradual adoption ("Try rotating just the tomato")
3. Predictive pest warnings ("Clubroot appeared 3 years after I planted cabbage here")

**Minimum data to collect for mixed beds:**
- Plant family (not just species name)
- Harvest date (when was the family removed)
- Pest/disease observed (if any)
- Season/year

## Section 6: Cover Crops & Green Manures for Atlantic Spain

### Why Cover Crops Matter in Atlantic Climate

**Benefits:**
1. Prevent leaching in high-rainfall zones (cover roots capture nitrogen before winter rains)
2. Improve soil structure (root systems break compaction, add organic matter)
3. Suppress weeds (living mulch effect)
4. Break pest cycles (non-host crops)

### Best Cover Crops for Atlantic Spain/UK

**Cool-Season (Plant Sep-Oct, grow through winter, terminate Mar-Apr)**

| Crop | Botanical Family | Benefit | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Winter rye** (Secale cereale) | Poaceae | Deep roots, high C/N, breaks hardpan | Allelopathic; suppress with caution |
| **Hairy vetch** (Vicia villosa) | Fabaceae | Nitrogen fixer, winter-hardy | Soft; easy to dig in; excellent choice |
| **Crimson clover** (Trifolium incarnatum) | Fabaceae | Nitrogen fixer, pretty, edible | Flowers attract pollinators early spring |
| **Field beans** (Vicia faba, Faba bona) | Fabaceae | Nitrogen fixer, grows large | Can be eaten; nitrogen rich residue |
| **Winter wheat** | Poaceae | Biomass, structure improvement | Non-legume; use if rotation needs non-N fixer |
| **Mustard** (Sinapis alba) | Brassicaceae | Biofumigant, pest suppression | Allelopathic; contains glucosinolates; terminate before seeding |

**Warm-Season (Plant May-Jun, grow through summer, terminate Aug-Sep)**

| Crop | Botanical Family | Benefit | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Buckwheat** (Fagopyrum) | Polygonaceae | Phosphorus mobilizer, pollinator attractor, fast-growing | Shallow roots; quick turnaround (8 weeks) |
| **Borage** (Borago officinalis) | Boraginaceae | Pollinator attractor, edible flowers, pest repellent | Self-seeds; reappears following year |
| **Sunflower** (Helianthus annuus) | Asteraceae | Phytoremediation (draws toxins), deep roots, structure | Allelopathic; can suppress next crop slightly |

### Rotation-Integrated Cover Crop Strategy

**For 3-4 bed rotation, use cover crops as "transition phases":**

**Bed A Year 1:** Heavy feeders (brassicas) → Harvest Oct/Nov → Sow winter rye/clover (Nov) → Terminate (Mar) → Plant legumes (Apr)

**Bed B Year 1:** Legumes (peas, beans) → Harvest July → Sow buckwheat (July) → Terminate (Sept) → Plant alliums (Oct)

**This gives the app a 4-phase bed tracking system:**
1. Active crop (with pest/disease history)
2. Cover crop (transition/building phase)
3. Fallow/green manure (terminated, decomposing)
4. Preparation (awaiting next crop)

### Cover Crop Recommendations (Filtered by Region)

**For Asturias/Atlantic Spain specifically:**
- Best winter crop: **Hairy vetch** (Vicia villosa) - hardy, nitrogen-fixing, dies back in late spring
- Best summer crop: **Buckwheat** - quick, improves phosphorus, breaks quickly
- Avoid in wet zones: **Rye** - can be hard to terminate in wet clay
- Edible option: **Crimson clover** (spring leaves) or **field beans** (whole crop)

```
plant_species table:
```

```
  {
    "diseases": ["clubroot", "late_blight"],

    "notes": "Severe blight in August"
```

```
grow_rotation_plans table:
- id (uuid, PK)

- (user_id, bed_id, target_year)
```

**4. Cover Crop Tracking (extend `grow_bed_plantings`)**

```
grow_bed_plantings table additions:
- is_cover_crop (boolean, default false)

- nitrogen_added_kg_per_sqm (numeric, calculated: 1-3 kg/sqm typical for legume cover)
```

### Rotation Recommendation Engine (API Logic)

**Endpoint: `/api/grow/beds/[bedId]/rotation-suggestions`**

**Input:**
- `bed_id` (uuid)
- `current_year` (integer)
- `rotation_scheme` (enum: '3_year', '4_year', '2_bed_simple')

**Logic:**

1. **Query planting history** for this bed (last 5 years)
   ```sql
   SELECT plant_family, removed_at
   FROM grow_bed_plantings
   WHERE bed_id = ?
   ORDER BY removed_at DESC
   LIMIT 10
   ```

2. **Identify last 3 families** planted

3. **Determine forbidden families** based on rules:
   - If last family is Solanaceae: forbid Solanaceae for 4 years (5 in Atlantic climate)
   - If last family is Brassicaceae: forbid for 3 years minimum
   - If last family is Allium: forbid for 2 years

4. **Suggest optimal next family** based on scheme and history

5. **Return suggestions with reasoning:**
   ```json
   {
     "current_family": "Solanaceae",
     "last_planted": "2025-08-15",
     "suggestion": "Legume (Fabaceae)",
     "reason": "Solanaceae in last 3 years. Legume will restore nitrogen and break pest cycle.",
     "compatibility": 9,
     "avoid": ["Solanaceae", "Cucurbitaceae"],
     "cover_crop_suggestion": {
       "name": "Hairy vetch",
       "family": "Fabaceae",
       "plant_date": "2025-11-01",
       "terminate_date": "2026-03-15"
     }
   }
   ```

**Key insight for home gardeners:** A simple 3-year or 4-year rotation prevents 80% of soil-borne pest/disease problems. Perfect is not necessary; consistency matters more.

**Key insight for Atlantic climate:** Blight and clubroot are the main concerns. Solanaceae (4-5 year gap) and Brassicaceae (3 year gap) are non-negotiable in wet regions. Legume nitrogen fixation is a real, measurable benefit when legumes precede heavy feeders.

## Appendix: Common Questions & Answers

**Q: Do I really need to rotate if I add compost every year?**
A: Compost helps, but doesn't eliminate soil-borne pathogens (they survive in soil structure). Rotation is biological control; compost is soil nutrition. Both work together, neither alone is sufficient.

**Q: What if I only have 2 beds?**
A: Use the 2-bed simple 3-year rotation, with seasonal variety (spring legume + summer heavy feeder in same bed, different families). Or embrace mixed planting with zone rotation within beds.

**Q: Can I skip rotation if I have no pests?**
A: Rotation is also about soil nutrition and structure, not just pests. Brassicas exhaust nitrogen; legumes restore it. Skip rotation, and you'll gradually deplete soil even without visible pests. Give rotation 3 years before judging effectiveness.

**Q: Buckwheat, clover, vetch — which cover crop is "best"?**
A: Depends on timing. Winter rains? Hairy vetch (nitrogen + hardy). Summer gap? Buckwheat (fast, phosphorus mobilizer). Want edible? Field beans or crimson clover. No wrong choice; consistency matters.

**Q: Should I rotate in containers (not raised beds)?**
A: Yes, but simplified. Use the "family per container" rule. Swap top 5-10 cm of soil annually, add compost. Or move pots to different locations to vary light/drainage.

**Q: What if I have a patio with pots, not beds?**
A: Track plant families in pots by moving pots between locations or refreshing soil. Rotation is less critical in containers (soil is fresh each year) than in permanent beds, but still beneficial.

**Q: Garlic in October, tomato in May — same bed, OK?**
A: Yes! Different seasons, different families. Just ensure garlic is removed before tomato roots get competitive (May is fine). This is "smart seasonal rotation."
